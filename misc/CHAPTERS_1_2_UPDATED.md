# CHAPTER ONE
## INTRODUCTION

The rapid growth of the number of IoT devices has dramatically changed the way the world connects and communicates with the digital world. They exist in almost every place of our life, from the smart homes and the wearable health tracker in our wrist to industrial control systems, smart city etc. With billions of devices connected worldwide generating, transferring and processing petabytes of data each second, the environment of IoT technology offers incredible benefits and chances for the development of the efficiency, automation and the intelligent of the devices, but on the other hand, its high interconnection nature has brought an undeniable security problem, and such security problems are threatening the validity of the IoT system. The identification of unusual or malignant behavior patterns in a network, namely network anomaly detection, has become one of the most critical protective tools for defense against the wide and evolving variety of cyber-attacks to IoT systems.

This chapter provides the necessary foundation of this research of a Lightweight GNN-Based Anomaly Detection system targeting the restricted IoT devices. This chapter begins with providing a general background covering the development of IoT, its security issues, and the development trend of machine learning techniques applied on network traffic analysis, it then outlines the specific research problem the work aims to solve, states the objectives and research questions, elaborates the importance and scope of the research, and finishes with the arrangement of the research work.

### 1.1 Background of the Study

The Internet of Things (IoT) is arguably one of the most revolutionary technological paradigm shifts of the twenty-first century. The core idea behind IoT is that physical devices ranging from sensors, actuators and embedded systems, to everyday consumer electronics like smart phones, to large-scale industrial and medical devices like heavy machinery and patient monitors, can be networked together to gather, transmit, and take action on data automatically with little or no human input. The term, coined by Kevin Ashton in 1999 as part of supply chain management, has grown dramatically to become part of all kinds of fields including agriculture, healthcare, transportation, manufacturing and urban planning. It is estimated by industry forecasts that over 15 billion connected IoT devices were active worldwide in 2023, and the number is expected to grow to more than 29 billion by the end of the decade, presenting the largest digital nervous system the world has ever seen.

This growth has been propelled by the parallel advent of many technological enablers, such as ubiquitous and ultra-low latency 5G communication networks, a decreasing cost for microcontroller chips and sensor hardware, improved edge computing architectures, and maturing wireless communication protocols such as Zigbee, Z-Wave, LoRaWAN and NB-IoT. Collectively, these enablers reduce the overall technical complexity and financial burden of deploying IoT at scale. Governments and organizations worldwide are able to instrument their physical environment – thousands of sensors to monitor traffic patterns, air pollution and power grids in a city, or wearable sensors to transmit vital health statistics and patient readings to healthcare providers – that would previously have been cost or technically infeasible (Thein et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024).

Despite these advancements, IoT networks inherently have structural security vulnerabilities. Most IoT devices are designed to comply with strict physical Size, Weight, Power, and Cost constraints-abbreviated as SWAP-C constraints for the embedded systems design field. Such a combined constraint dictates that a typical IoT endpoint uses microcontrollers that possess relatively low speeds of central processing unit (CPU) (typically ranging from 8 to 32 MHz) as well as memory storage capacities in the range of kilobytes for RAM and flash memory rather than gigabytes. Power consumption is another very strict constraint on many IoT devices; many IoT devices use batteries and are meant to run from months to many years without replacement. It is, therefore, impractical for these devices to be equipped with the computationally demanding and computationally expensive security features typically available in traditional servers and enterprise endpoints (e.g., full scale anti-virus, hardware accelerated encryption or deep packet inspection firewalls) (Alwaisi & Soderi, 2024).

The impact of insecure IoT devices is widespread and has already resulted in several notorious attacks. Mirai botnet of 2016 had compromised hundreds of thousands of devices like digital cameras, routers and digital video recorders using default credentials to launch one of the largest distributed denials of service (DDoS) attacks the internet had ever seen. Such an attack crippled major internet sites for a day. Cyber-attack on smart healthcare devices has also brought up security concerns on the safety of patients when attackers were able to remotely modify dose setting on a connected insulin pump and pacemaker (Villegas-Ch et al., 2025). In industrial operational technology (OT) networks, a compromise of IoT sensors connected within an OT network has led to manipulation of physical processes, with one attack on a water treatment plant being reported as having led to modification of water treatment parameters, while a different attack successfully breached industrial power grid operations.

It is impossible to overstate the importance of IoT security, and specifically the critical role of anomaly detection in IoT networks, both in research and practical applications. Network-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) represent the most common technical means to detect unwanted or anomalous activity within a network. There are two common types of IDS, each with its pros and cons: signature-based detection, which involves comparing incoming network traffic to an established database of known attack "signatures", and anomaly-based detection, which monitors deviations from a learned "normal" network behavior. Signature-based systems have very high precision and low false positive rates against known attacks but lack the ability to detect zero-day attacks and variations, and this limitation becomes critical in the rapidly evolving and diverse attack landscape for IoT devices (Villegas-Ch et al., 2025).

In recent years, researchers have investigated the use of machine learning and deep learning techniques in building sophisticated and generalized anomaly detection systems for IoT network traffic to circumvent the limitations of traditional IDS systems. Early work applied classical machine learning techniques such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Decision Tree, Random Forest and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) to handcrafted features extracted from raw network packet data. Research by Kurniabudi et al. (2019) found that ensemble techniques like Random Forest could provide highly accurate detection performance onbenchmark datasets like UNSW-NB15 and KDD Cup 99, establishing the capabilities of such models. However, these techniques rely on the laborious and dataset-specific task of feature engineering-handcrafted extraction and selection of important information features from raw packet-level network traffic-before feeding data into a classifier.

This process was largely automated by deep learning models which were applied to automatic feature extraction from raw data representations. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) were among the first deep architectures applied, and can naturally model temporal dependencies in time-series network traffic flows. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were adapted to model network traffic as 2-D data and employ image classification techniques on byte-stream representations of network packets; such models were able to capture much higher-level features than classical machine learning approaches. More advanced models like attention-based methods and transformers have more recently been studied, extending on deep learning's ability to automatically extract features that capture long-term dependencies in network sessions (Carletti et al., 2025).

Of particular interest is the adaptation of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to the task of network intrusion detection. GNNs are deep learning models that learn from data where entities are connected by relationship, i.e., graph-structured data. Network traffic is intrinsically graph-structured: individual devices are nodes in the graph, communication flows are the edges connecting them, and the topology between the nodes and edges conveys a great amount of information about normal operation as well as attack patterns. Deng et al. (2022) proposed dynamic graphs to model network traffic where edges are weighted based on traffic metrics such as the number of packets, volume, or protocol, allowing GNNs to simultaneously capture both the low-level structural context of individual device communications, and the higher-level patterns in the overall network topology, providing the potential for detecting complex, multi-hop attack schemes which would otherwise remain hidden in simpler traffic statistics.

Various architectures under the GNN umbrella have been employed successfully in the realm of network security. GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE were all tested on an IoT security benchmark with accuracy rates above 98% on datasets such as IoT-23 and IoTID20. (Villegas-Ch et al., 2025; Carletti et al., 2025). The IoT-23 dataset was released by Stratosphere Lab at the Czech Technical University and is a collection of network traffic captures from 23 different IoT malware scenarios along with normal traffic which made it a useful dataset for IoT specific anomaly detection research. It is considered one of the most extensive public datasets for this purpose. Similar to the IoT-23 dataset, the IoTID20 dataset provides labeled flow level records that are both attack and normal traffic from a smart home setting. These datasets serve as a valuable tool for comparing GNNs against each other as well as other traditional algorithms like Random Forest and SVM.

More recently, topology-adaptive GNN architectures have been developed to adapt the structure of the graph during inference based on dynamic communication patterns (Zhou et al., 2024). These networks are reported to be top-performing in both detection accuracy and their ability to adapt to unseen attack scenarios. Other recent works utilize GNNs with federated learning to learn a global detection model over multiple distributed IoT gateways without needing to transmit raw traffic (Villegas-Ch et al., 2025). This research highlights the potential of GNNs for on-device IoT security by mitigating concerns over bandwidth and data privacy.

Despite their promising performance and expanding research scope, most of the GNN-based anomaly detection systems proposed so far are intended for implementation on high-performance computing environments such as cloud servers and edge gateways that have sufficient hardware capacity, including multiple cores, GPUs, and gigabytes of RAM. A multi-layer GNN with many graph convolutions can be computationally intensive due to the numerous matrix multiplications and neighborhood aggregation operations that scale with both the number of nodes in the graph and the depth of the graph. Furthermore, memory consumption will similarly increase as both adjacency matrix and node feature matrix are required. This would simply not be a viable option for low-power, resource-constrained IoT edge devices like a Raspberry Pi Zero or ARM Cortex-M microcontroller (Alwaisi & Soderi, 2024; Zhou et al., 2024).

In a recent survey on lightweight deep learning models for IoT anomaly detection, Zang (2023) identified that an optimized GNN algorithm that is suitable for IoT edge devices is still missing in the field. While model pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation have been well explored with CNNs and RNNs, corresponding techniques are relatively new for the graph-structured computation in GNNs. Model distillation, for example, where a complex, high-performing "teacher" network is trained to mimic a lightweight "student" network has proved successful in shrinking deep learning models without significant accuracy degradation and there are still very few research contributions on this technique applied to GNNs for IoT security (Deng et al., 2022). Topology-adaptive graph convolution layers also offer an unexplored direction of optimizing GNNs for low-resource settings where the network computes neighbor information based on most relevant neighborhoods rather than aggregate neighbor information. (Zhou et al., 2024).

A major gap that remains unaddressed in the existing literature is the lack of extensive benchmarking for GNN inference latency and memory usage on real, resource-constrained hardware. While many prior works focus on accuracy and are concerned with F1-scores and ROC-AUC values, few pay attention to performance evaluation parameters such as inference time per sample, RAM consumption, number of parameters, and energy consumption per inference, which are crucial for any model targeting resource-constrained IoT edge devices (Ahanger et al., 2025; Alwaisi & Soderi, 2024). This gap between laboratory evaluation metrics and real-world deployment feasibility makes it hard to compare different GNN based security solutions against each other, and also a major roadblock towards utilizing GNNs in the IoT edge environment.

The motivation behind this research comes from the urgent need for IoT security at both national and global level. Regulatory frameworks like the EU Cyber Resilience Act (2024) and the US IoT Cybersecurity Improvement Act (2020) have mandated minimal security requirements for IoT devices. These laws clearly signal the importance of on-device security features as it is expected that security on IoT devices will become a legal necessity rather than an optional feature in the near future. Alongside, the green AI paradigm brings awareness towards energy footprint and there is a need to develop highly accurate GNN architectures that have minimal energy consumption and are computationally efficient (Alwaisi & Soderi, 2024). Therefore, the goal of this research is to develop a GNN-based anomaly detection framework that can work efficiently on low-power IoT edge devices without compromising the detection accuracy and speed.

### 1.2 Problem Statement

Current Graph Neural Network based solutions for IoT anomaly detection require high-performance server and cloud hardware, thus are impractical for resource-constrained IoT edge devices which have very low CPU speeds, kilobyte amounts of RAM and power limitations.

In the current state, the vast IoT ecosystem consists of billions of resource-constrained heterogeneous IoT devices, that serve as backbone for crucial application domains such as healthcare, industrial automation, smart city, and consumer devices. The exponential growth of this ecosystem comes with the increased exposure of IoT devices to a multitude of network attacks such as DoS, Man-in-the-Middle, Botnet, Port Scanning, etc., whose successful attacks can result in service disruption, financial loss or threaten human lives and critical infrastructure (Villegas-Ch et al., 2025; Thein et al., 2023). Earlier research moved from traditional IDS and ML-based models towards DL based models, among which GNNs are best suited for modeling graph-structured network traffic. Several researchers such as Deng et al., 2022, Zhou et al., 2024, and Villegas-Ch et al., 2025 have shown accuracy greater than 95% in detection rate of multi-step attack scenarios on IoT specific benchmark datasets using various GNN architectures. The main drawback of these studies is that all their work are evaluated on high-performance computer environments, but without proof of deployment feasibility in the limited IoT edge device environment. Zang (2023) concluded that an optimized lightweight data collection and processing pipeline for IoT edge deployment is missing, and a study from Alwaisi and Soderi (2024) indicated that existing DL methods pose high memory and computation overhead which cannot be supported by the limited resource constraints of IoT edge devices.

This is the core gap that this research aims to fill, i.e., the absence of a dedicated GNN-based anomaly detection framework that has been evaluated on real IoT malware traffic and assessed for computational and memory efficiency under the constraints that IoT edge devices operate within. The full potential of GNNs, due to their remarkable detection performance, is currently only realizable in laboratory and cloud settings; thus billions of resource-constrained IoT devices in the field can benefit from graph aware and topology sensitive anomaly detection systems. This gap must be filled as the continuous expansion of IoT ecosystem, the increasing sophistication of attacks against IoT systems, and evolving regulatory demands for on-device security makes a low power and resource efficient anomaly detection system essential (Ahanger et al., 2025; Alwaisi & Soderi, 2024).

### 1.3 Research Aim

The primary aim of this research is to design, implement, and evaluate a lightweight GNN-based anomaly detection framework for detecting malicious traffic in IoT networks, built and trained without reliance on high-performance cloud or server-class infrastructure, and to assess its resource efficiency against the published hardware profile of a representative resource-constrained IoT edge device, the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W.

### 1.4 Objectives

1. To represent IoT network traffic in a graph-based format by converting raw flow-level records from the IoT-23 benchmark dataset into graphs where each IoT device is a node and communications between two IoT devices form weighted edges, with node-level features (degree, centrality measures, clustering coefficient, and temporal neighbour-change ratios) computed from the same flow records.

2. To develop and implement a lightweight GNN architecture suitable for edge deployment, constructing a shallow, two-layer graph convolutional autoencoder with a minimal, explicitly stated parameter count, achieving a small memory and computational footprint through architectural minimalism, and to identify quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation as compression techniques for extending this architecture further in future work.

3. To evaluate the anomaly detection performance of the developed model using Precision, Recall, and F1-Score against real, ground-truth labelled IoT network traffic, covering botnet communication and port-scanning behaviour, and to compare the model's performance against classical anomaly detection baselines — a statistical rule-based detector, Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, and One-Class SVM — evaluated on identical graph features.

4. To assess the resource efficiency of the proposed model through analytical resource profiling: measuring the model's parameter count, model size, and per-node inference latency on development hardware, and evaluating this footprint against the published memory specification of the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W as a representative resource-constrained IoT edge device.

5. To propose deployment guidelines for IoT system developers, network security experts and embedded system engineers on acceptable model architecture, graph-building parameters, and detection thresholds, based on the detection performance and resource assessment obtained in this project, and to identify physical hardware validation as the next stage toward full deployment guidance.

### 1.5 Significance of the Study

The significance of this research is multifold from practical, academic, societal, and policy perspectives.

**Practical Contribution.** The proposed lightweight GNN-based anomaly detection framework provides a security tool that can be directly deployed by an organization or individual who owns and manages IoT fleet. It will enable anomaly detection at or near the IoT device itself, instead of transmitting raw network telemetry data to a distant cloud server for processing; thus, reduces latency of detection, less bandwidth overhead and protect the privacy of the network telemetry by avoiding transferring sensitive data to a third party's cloud service. Organizations that are managing IoT deployments in environments sensitive to latency and with limited bandwidth like industrial control rooms, agricultural IoT devices deployed in remote locations and medical systems, are directly benefited by on-device anomaly detection (Zang, 2023; Villegas-Ch et al., 2025).

**Academic Contribution.** This research contributes to the body of knowledge in both the area of deep learning on graphs and security of embedded systems. It proposes a graph construction approach and a lightweight, minimally-parameterized GNN architecture, evaluated against classical anomaly detection baselines on real, labelled IoT malware traffic. It contributes an analytical method for assessing a graph model's resource footprint against a stated target-device profile, useful in contexts where physical hardware access is limited — a step toward the systematic latency/memory evaluation the literature identifies as missing, discussed further in Chapter Two. The work also provides useful insights to the "green AI" literature which suggests achieving high accuracy for anomaly detection doesn't always mean higher computational cost (Alwaisi & Soderi, 2024; Carletti et al., 2025).

**Societal Contribution.** This work makes a direct contribution to the protection of critical infrastructure and services on which millions of people rely every day, e.g., secure smart cities, reliable smart medical systems and automated industrial processes. Reduced False Positive rate also significantly lessens the burden of security analysts who are struggling with alert fatigue of today's conventional IDS that produces thousands of false positive alarms per day (Carletti et al., 2025).

**Policy Contribution.** This research has relevance to the policy discussion around upcoming IoT security legislations such as the EU Cyber Resilience Act and the IoT cybersecurity frameworks enacted by various countries, which require the presence of certain basic security on every IoT device. The evidence produced in this work — a working lightweight detector, its measured detection performance on real IoT malware traffic, and its analytically-assessed resource footprint — contributes indicative evidence toward the feasibility of lightweight on-device anomaly detection on resource-constrained IoT devices. Future students and developers can build on the models and pipelines produced by this project, including carrying its resource assessment forward into physical hardware validation.

### 1.6 Scope and Delimitations

The scope of this research is limited in multiple dimensions to fit within the context of a final year undergraduate project with available resources and timeframe. Specifically, the study concentrates on network-based anomaly detection using graph-based learning methods on IoT network traffic.

**Subject Coverage:** The research analyzes bi-directional traffic flows and inter-device communication patterns among IoT entities captured in real, publicly available network traffic datasets (Thein et al., 2023; Villegas-Ch et al., 2025). The proposed system is evaluated against a real, labelled IoT malware capture exhibiting horizontal port-scanning behaviour, and against a real, labelled general (non-IoT) botnet capture from the related CTU-13 dataset, which tests the same architecture's generalization beyond the IoT domain. Physical layer attacks, radio frequency fingerprinting, and side-channel attacks are not investigated, as they are not applicable to a software-based solution operating on flow-level records. Host-based intrusion detection focusing on analysis of system call, log files and processes execution within a device is beyond the scope due to the lack of system access on a typically locked-down IoT system. Application layer attacks such as encrypted HTTPS/DTLS traffic would require decryption keys which poses legal and privacy concerns (Carletti et al., 2025).

**Data Sources:** This study relies on real, labelled network captures — a scenario from the IoT-23 dataset (Stratosphere Lab, Czech Technical University) for the primary IoT evaluation, and a scenario from the related CTU-13 dataset for a supplementary, non-IoT evaluation of the same architecture. Both are converted from their original flow-record formats into a graph representation using a reproducible conversion pipeline built for this project. Actual live traffic capture and on-device testing using physical IoT devices are beyond this project's resources and timeframe.

Resource efficiency is assessed analytically: the model's parameter count, memory footprint, and per-node inference latency are measured on development hardware, then evaluated against the published specification of the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W (512 MB RAM, quad-core ARM Cortex-A53) as a representative resource-constrained IoT edge device. Physical deployment and empirical on-device measurement are identified in Chapter Five as the next stage of this work.

The lightweight GNN architecture is implemented directly in a general-purpose deep learning framework (PyTorch), keeping the software dependency footprint minimal and installable without compiled, architecture-specific extensions. The architectural exploration in this project centres on a single Graph Convolutional Network variant, implemented directly rather than through a graph-specific library.

# CHAPTER TWO
## LITERATURE REVIEW

### 2.0 INTRODUCTION

This chapter review theory, methods, operations and architectures regarding finding security threat of the Internet of Things (IoT). As deep learning capabilities spread over the current everyday smart devices, performing them efficiently under resource constraint hardware is crucial. This survey forms an intuitive bridge between IoT structure, device resource constraint, GNN and Graph Theory for handling network traffic problems. Meanwhile it differentiates the common edge-fog-cloud setup and decentralised federated frames, illustrates limitations and research gaps found in recent studies by investigating the methods on model pruning and evaluations, such as ignore resource effectiveness, lack of structural standards, disregard for real-world rules, and indicates a clear direction to design lightweight and environmental friendly intelligent defense systems.

### 2.1.1 The Internet of Things (IoT): Definition, Architecture and Evolution

The Internet of Things (IoT) is a set of connected objects having their respective processing capabilities, communicating through internet protocol via a variety of sensors and actuating mechanisms. The progression of IoT has evolved over time from simple RFID tracking tag to a sophisticated cyber-physical system with multi-tier structure. Commonly, edge-fog-cloud framework offers a multi-tier approach to handle heterogeneous and mass volume data generated from numerous devices; nevertheless the network's open communication protocols and sensitive data held by diverse device types expose network to extensive threat possibilities. Evidence from recent development indicate a problem: even as simple devices require substantial security protection to be secure from multi-stage attacks, the scattered network structure lacks a universal and uniform security administration policy. Investigation of structural flaws thus becomes an essential step to devise context-aware automated detection models applicable for various circumstances.

### 2.1.2 Hardware Resource Constraints of IoT Devices-SWAP-C Model

IoT devices operate within relatively strict Size, Weight, Area, Power and Cost (SWAP-C) limits. In contrast with enterprise level computers, microcontrollers and embedded processors used in IoT devices have small size, limited memory and storage space, small power supplies, etc. Because of such tight constraints, software executed locally on these devices needs to be small and uncomplicated. Furthermore, security patches that are often large, cannot easily be deployed onto an IoT device; it might consume excessive amounts of power, depleting the battery, or over-load the device, thus causing system crash. Due to those constraints, it becomes unfeasible to directly port general heavy encryption algorithm and deep learning models onto constrained small IoT devices. In brief, reducing algorithmic complexity while maintaining high defense power is the key. Security research must look at the SWAP-C constraints while evaluating the performance of the algorithms/models.

### 2.1.3 Network Anomaly Detection: concept and classification

The purpose of network anomaly detection is to establish a pattern of traffic that differs significantly from the normal functioning baseline. The classic classification method classifies detection methods into signature-based, specification-based, and anomaly-based frameworks. Since it can detect novel attacks and zero-day threats without the signature of known attacks, anomaly detection has great advantages over existing intrusion detection systems. But differentiating the malicious traffic from the normal behavioral changes of a network is a complex task. The high false alert rates usually hinder the normal network operations and make the security officers' warning fatigued. And under the heterogeneous networks, defining the precise boundaries of normal behavior is difficult due to the fluctuating network traffic, the diverse functions of the devices, and the existing noise. So the state of the art methods focus on creating adaptable frameworks that take both structural and behavioral patterns into consideration to lower the number of errors and achieve high detection accuracy.

### 2.1.4 Network traffic described using Graph Theory concepts

Using Graph Theory to model network traffic is a very robust approach, considering each communication device a node, and data exchanged between two nodes an edge, which is directed and weighted. By representing the serial of packet streams this way, we create a comprehensive map of the network, depicting layout relationships totally lost in a simple table format. Critical topological features like degree distributions, centrality scores or community cluster structure can be extracted to identify underlying attack forms, e.g. Coordinated distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) or horizontal movement in a network. The study of network graphs in terms of structure, enables security systems to observe a large amount of communication flowing over several paths simultaneously. This Graph-based approach changes network traffic inspection from a package-centric to a relation-centric perspective and is therefore useful in detecting stealthy multi-step attacks, trying to mimic benign behavior in single packet analysis.

### 2.1.5 Graph Neural Networks (GNN)

**Basics.** Graph neural networks (GNNs) enable deep learning to work on non-Euclidean graph data and represent a new milestone for deep learning. By following either neighborhood aggregation or message passing schemes, GNNs transform node feature representations by collecting and transforming the structure information localized to individual nodes as vectors states from neighboring nodes. In this way, it is able to learn node-level features as well as graph-level structure concurrently, so as to produce useful node embeddings for subsequent classification. In the cybersecurity context, GNNs leverage the structural information to identify complex network issues by examining relationship links between connected components. But these steps demand much memory bandwidth and high computation speed of floating-point operations throughout the matrix multiplication processes. Having this fundamental computation nature in mind is critical to resolve the trade-off between demanding computation nature of GNN and limited hardware constraints on the edge network.

### 2.1.6 Lightweight and Green AI

Lightweight and Green AI concepts promote a complete redesign of deep learning models aiming to achieve both a lower environmental footprint and reduce computational resources, thereby enabling reduced power consumption, without significant loss in prediction accuracy. Driven by the global objective of green sustainability and hardware limitations, Green AI suggests changing the design focus of the algorithm away from mere performance metrics toward practical operational efficiency. The development of small, optimized neural structures suitable for local operation on device edges is thus encouraged through a number of design techniques such as extra parameter reduction, quantization, and building efficient layer structures. Within a distributed system, it enables constant security verification on the edge nodes while averting excessive system load and power consumption. Building compact deep learning models for the network edge to constitute permanently self-sustaining security systems is finally an essential requirement.

### 2.2.1 Signature Based Traditional IDS

Existing traditional signature-based IDS, such as Snort or Suricata use predetermined databases of known attacks pattern, byte sequence and threat hashes to locate security violations. Each incoming packet is compared with saved signature and once match is found, an alert is generated. Traditional signature-based ID systems are effective to discover the known attacks with minimum processing delay and minimum false alarm rate, however it has several limitations, such as fixed design of the signature based systems, that are unaware about novel zero-day attacks, evolving variants of malwares and tailor made exploitation tactics. Additionally, maintaining the large and up to date signature database consume significant memory and network bandwidth which is not favorable for edge device with constrained resources. Therefore using only signature based ID may leave the distributed network vulnerable to the new fast growing attacks.

### 2.2.2 Cloud-based and central Anomaly detection platforms

These platforms resolve on-premise hardware restrictions by redirecting deep learning processing onto remote high-power data centers. In this arrangement, distributed edge nodes just act as data gatherers. Instead, all raw network logs are streamed to a central cloud server, where they are fully analyzed. This arrangement permits us to employ more computationally complex, albeit extremely precise deep learning models; but the operational drawbacks are significant. This process is characterized by high bandwidth consumption that inevitably produces data delays and delays real-time threat countermeasures. It also presents significant data privacy issues as it consolidates critical data into one place; and the system itself is extremely vulnerable to a single point of failure - any failure in connection to the central cloud renders edge networks vulnerable.

### 2.2.3 Edge and Federated Intrusion Detection Systems

Edge and federated IDS provide a robust, decentralized approach, distributing the anomaly detection mechanisms right on the local edge nodes (gateway nodes). In a federated scenario, each edge node performs local model training using its own local data. The parameters, not the data, are then sent to the coordinator. The coordinator aggregates the parameters, then aggregates these parameters into a single, shared global model which is then sent to all of the edge nodes. This approach not only secures sensitive user data by allowing it to stay localized, but also reduces the burden on network resources. Challenges of federated approach includes difference in hardware on edge nodes and the timing delay produced due to differing memory size and processing speed. Dealing with varying training speeds while still keeping the local model small enough for small hardware is a major engineering problem.

### 2.2.4 Key IoT attack incidents and their detection implications

The history of IoT attack incidents reflects how serious a global problem distributed vulnerabilities can pose and the imperative to improve our infrastructures. Well-known attacks such as the Mirai botnet took advantage of a multitude of cheap consumer electronics and weak default factory passwords to launch wide scale distributed denial-of-service attacks. Mirai was followed by a more advanced set of botnets, Hajime and Mozi, that demonstrated sophisticated self-sufficient peer-to-peer structures capable of independent propagation and resistance to centrally managed shutdown attempts. These incidents illustrate severe shortcomings of traditional defense strategies: traditional signature-based defense mechanisms were unable to keep up with dynamic malware types, while traditional centralized detection engines were too slow and computationally limited to process large quantities of attack data. Effective defenses to modern automated attacks must therefore incorporate self-sufficient, smart anomaly detection schemes capable of identifying suspect activity, particularly lateral movement at the individual device level.

### 2.3.1 IoT Communication Protocols and their security profiles

The varying connectivity needs of the IoT network have led to the design of several tailored communication protocols operating on various layers of network communication that feature diverse security profiles. Lightweight messaging protocols like MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport) and CoAP (Constrained Application Protocol) operate on the application layer and utilize a publish-subscribe model, limiting communication overhead. Operating at the network and physical layers, protocols like 6LoWPAN, Zigbee, and LoRaWAN are developed for low-power, long-range communication of small data packets for small hardware nodes. Although these protocols feature excellent efficiency characteristics, the security characteristics of these protocols are often compromised since they are devoid of heavy encryption to save power. The consequence is an open channel where traffic may be subject to packet spoofing, selective forwarding, and Man-In-The-Middle (MITM) attacks. To build efficient anomaly detection mechanisms, one must be intimately aware of these protocol structures to be able to distinguish malicious exploits from legitimate traffic.

### 2.3.2 Edge Computing and Embedded Hardware Platforms

Edge computing shifts network architectures to offload processing tasks from remote clouds to embedded hardware devices on-site. Current edge hardware ranges from inexpensive, severely resource-limited microcontrollers (such as the ARM Cortex-M series) to powerful embedded devices (such as the Raspberry Pi and the NVIDIA Jetson modules). In fact, most embedded systems nowadays include dedicated neural processing units (NPUs) or hardware accelerators in order to accelerate matrix arithmetic operations. Nonetheless, power constraints and hardware cost bounds require most commonly encountered edge deployments to use modest microcontrollers with relatively constrained power consumption and with almost no RAM (just a couple of kilobytes) along with lack of hardware acceleration for demanding arithmetic operations. Therefore, security engineers are required to closely align the software specifications and hardware resources to ensure the system is not overloaded.

### 2.3.3 Benchmark IoT Datasets: IoT-23 and IoTID20

Anomalous deep learning models need representative, high-quality training data which accurately describe network normal behavior as well as modern attacks. Old networks, such as KDD Cup 99 or NSL-KDD, do not include real-time special protocol mixtures and traffic specific patterns like they are now. Two newer sets, namely IoT-23 and IoTID20, aim to fill this void and represent up to date network traffic and attack scenarios. They are composed of real recorded malicious traffic from botnets in the network, as well as useful operational traffic. However, they generally come in flat, tabular structures which demand much preprocessing in order to convert the text-based logs into a graph without losing its characteristic relationships.

### 2.3.4 Model Compression Techniques to enable edge deployment

Model compression is a set of techniques used to compress large, complex deep learning models to small, fast program. Main compression strategies include weight pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation and optimizing the network structure. Weight pruning helps to prune the irrelevant connections in neural network, leading to a lower memory usage. Quantization helps to reduce the precision of mathematics parameter (e.g., turn 32bit float value to 8bit integer value), which speed up the computation very greatly and reduces RAM usage. Knowledge distillation technique moves the prediction abilities of a large 'teacher' model to a smaller 'student' network. Such methods have been widely explored and utilized in traditional image and text processing tasks. However, they are quite hard to apply for graph based model structures, as it's too easy to disrupt complicated structural information in graph when modifying network weights.

### 2.3.5 Regulatory Framework

IoT security With the increasing popularity of interconnected devices, it has become imperative for authorities worldwide to establish legal and standardisation guidelines for IoT security such as Europe's Cyber Resilience Act, and the US' IoT Security Improvement Act, which mandates basic security principles, such as prohibiting the use of default passwords and enforcing a system for reporting vulnerabilities. These regulations require producers to be held legally accountable for the security features of their products. However, applying such stringent legal rules on a low cost embedded device presents enormous design challenges where designers must ensure that security aspects meet the legal standards within tight constraints of the hardware limitations.

### 2.4.1 Classical Machine Learning models for Network Anomaly Detection

Classical machine learning algorithms such as SVM, Random Forests, K-Nearest Neighbors, Isolation Forest are employed in network anomaly detection systems due to their predictable and fast computation. These algorithms extract meaningful information (packet number, port, transmission duration, etc.) from tabular network data logs, and can be run on edge gateways in limited resources environments and offers quick response times; however, the reliance on explicit feature engineering does require high domain expertise, and they often only perceive network logs as individual events in time, lacking the awareness of multi-stage network attack exploiting complex structural relationships among a set of events.

### 2.4.2 Deep Learning models for Network Traffic Analysis

MLPs, Deep Belief Networks and other deep learning models automate feature learning through their layered neural architectures. By routing input through many hidden layers, these models are able to learn intricate non-linear relationships within data with minimal to no manual feature engineering thereby identifying subtle anomalies with high accuracy. These deep architectures, however, are heavily resource intensive, both in terms of memory and computational resources, hence challenges arise when attempting to deploy on low powered edge nodes and thus a great research area is focusing on optimized deep learning networks.

### 2.4.3 Recurrent Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

Recurrent Neural Networks and LSTM networks are specifically designed to process streams of sequential data and are excellent at learning and modeling time series patterns such as traffic on a network over time. LSTMs include a set of 'gates' inside of their memory cell that govern what information enters or leaves, providing a long-term memory for the network, which is helpful at avoiding many typical training issues such as disappearing gradients. In applications where the network security application must examine network traffic over time to determine whether to block malicious patterns (such as slow-rate brute force or slow data exfiltration attempts) LSTMs perform well. Unfortunately, these LSTM computations cannot be run in parallel; therefore processing at high speed with lots of packets causes a delay, and the large memory required to keep track of internal states causes problems on the small amount of memory available in low-power edge gateways.

### 2.4.4 Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) on Byte-Stream Representations

The use of CNNs has become common in network anomaly detection where raw packet byte streams are represented as 2-D image-like matrices and analyzed by a CNN. These representations are not images in any functional sense, but are used such that local CNN filters may search over "images" of packets and byte streams to find spatial patterns related to common protocol structures and payloads of malicious and benign data, eliminating complex manual feature engineering entirely. CNNs can be readily accelerated by simple parallel hardware processing elements. These image based approaches fail to capture the interconnections between communication participants.

### 2.4.5 Transformer and Attention-Based Models

These are the latest generation of sequence modeling neural networks. Transformers replace RNNs with a novel architecture known as the 'self-attention' mechanism, which can look at relationships between all packets and communications simultaneously in a data stream. This allows complex and multi-stage attacks which span across large periods of traffic log data to be identified based on hidden correlations. Standard transformer models are incredibly complex and their computationally demanding "self-attention" feature is computationally intensive to an extreme. Standard transformers' require very high processing capabilities, cannot run on resource constrained edge hardware and need specialized low-power optimization.

### 2.4.6 Graph Neural Network Architectures for Intrusion Detection

Graph Neural Network (GNN) architectures approach network intrusion detection by modeling traffic as graph structures, rather than flat sequences or 2-D images, allowing them to more naturally consider the structural relationships between different communications and devices. GNNs can easily identify distributed botnets, lateral movement through networks and hidden probes. This type of information passes through simpler networks without identification. Their drawback is computational expense. The message passing calculation is complex, requires a lot of processing and also a lot of memory. Thus many optimization efforts are focused on making GNNs applicable to edge gateways.

### 2.4.7 Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)

Graph Convolutional Networks are an efficient and powerful architecture for processing data structures in the form of a graph. Their fundamental operation is the normalized averaging of neighbor states and combining that with a node's individual state. This localized operation allows for the identification of areas of compromised nodes in a system or even malicious network scanning behavior. Their difficulty comes in the large computational demands and the requirements for all graph data to be loaded at training time which makes them difficult to deploy on small, low-memory edge gateways.

### 2.4.8 Graph Attention Networks (GAT)

Graph Attention Networks incorporate a masked self-attention mechanism within the neighbor aggregation calculation. A GAT assigns different importance values to different neighbors during the aggregation of neighboring node features, meaning that it can weigh less important connections less, allowing for more focused anomaly detection of high-priority interactions over low-priority traffic patterns. However, the mechanism which computes attention weights adds to the computation cost, which makes GATs an even less desirable option for small edge gateways than their GCN counterparts.

### 2.4.9 GraphSAGE and Inductive Generalization

GraphSAGE (Sample and Aggregate) provides an efficient, inductive approach to generate node embeddings in large or dynamic graph networks. Rather than computing transformations on the global graph, GraphSAGE only samples a predetermined number of adjacent neighbors and aggregates them into node representations through uniform functions (like mean, LSTM or pooling) for updating them. The limited neighborhood computation required makes the complexity manageable and enables the model to generate representations for entirely unseen nodes (zero-shot generalization) without retraining the model globally. In terms of network security, this inductive capacity becomes a powerful tool as it enables a defense mechanism to assess new devices upon connecting to the network. However, the challenge is the sampling size-smaller sampling saves memory but omits critical communication links required for reliable anomaly detection.

### 2.5.1 Evaluation Limited to High-Performance Computing Environments

One of the major constraints of current cybersecurity research papers on GNNs is the lack of evaluation of the GNNs in realistic, not high-performance, computing environments. In the majority of research works, GNN models are tested on servers with high-performance CPUs and enterprise GPUs and enormous amount of memory that produces amazing accuracy scores but overlooks the real-life constraints of edge networks with extremely small RAM and computing power provided by cheap microcontrollers. While a high-accuracy GNN tested on a GPU server might perform exceptionally, it will most likely fail or cause major system delays on a tiny edge gateway. A critical need to evaluate models on low-power edge hardware exists to ensure practical usability of the security solutions.

### 2.5.2 Absence of Resource-Efficiency Metrics in GNN Security Research

Current research on GNN for cybersecurity has a strong emphasis on classification performance measures like accuracy, F1-score and false alarm rate and is rather careless about resource-efficiency metrics (like runtime memory utilization, CPU execution time and power consumption) on the edge hardware. Academic papers very seldom include these vital numbers that dictate if a security model can be practically deployed and operated. This makes a gap between theoretical GNN development and practical security model feasibility and highlights the requirement for an equally rigorous set of evaluation metrics that covers both classification accuracy and resource consumption on edge hardware.

### 2.5.3 Immature GNN Model Compression Pipeline for IoT Security

While weight pruning and quantization-general neural network compression techniques-are very mature, the GNN model compression pipeline for IoT security still needs significant development. Most generic compression pipelines are designed for regular grids, such as images and text networks, which do not work well with the irregular, non-Euclidean, structure of graphs and can easily destroy the communication links between the nodes that result in a dramatic decrease of accuracy. Furthermore, generic compression techniques seldom take into consideration protocol structures and variety of attacks represented in network traffic datasets. As a result, the area requires development of GNN specific compression techniques which are capable of producing small model sizes without affecting its security features.

### 2.5.4 Lack of Standardized Graph Construction Protocol for IoT Traffic

A prerequisite to successfully apply Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to network security is having a standard way to transform tabular log data from network traffic into a graph structure. Most researchers adopt their own completely arbitrary custom method of defining the node, edges, and node features. For example, in one study, IP addresses are identified as nodes and traffic flows as edges, while in other study subnets can serve as nodes and TCP sessions as edges. This leads to a difficulty of comparison of different GNN models and replication of research work as well as the long processing time of custom graph construction techniques making the on-the-fly analysis impossible. A well-defined standardized graph construction protocol needs to be established for enabling research and development at the intersection of IoT security and GNNs.

### 2.5.5 Deployment Feasibility vs. Regulatory Compliance Gap

There exists a gap between strict legal requirements on information security and practical feasibility to deploy the security model on low-power embedded hardware. The regulations like Europe's Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) mandate very thorough security logging and real-time reporting of threats on connected devices, but large amount of information logged and real-time threat processing usually requires high level of memory and computation power on an embedded device, hence exceeding its current capability. Security developers are left with two bad choices: either reduce the amount of logging and information processed to get a system that operates or put the hardware to such heavy workload that it leads to unstable operation and high battery consumption.

### 2.6 LITERATURE SUMMARY

In conclusion, the literature demonstrates that, while the Internet of Things is burgeoning within critical infrastructure, the highly multi-layered architecture it presupposes is prone to state-of-the-art multi-stage malicious attacks such as distributed botnets and zero-day attacks. Although signature-based Intrusion Detection Systems are entirely blind to new attacks and centralized cloud-centric approaches create considerable latency, consume extensive Wide Area Network bandwidth, and introduce considerable privacy concerns from user data, edge and federated learning approaches present a decentralized alternative. These decentralized architectures however remain physically constrained by the physical limits of the SWAP-C model. Although Graph Neural Networks are a highly successful mathematical construct for modeling complex non-linear relationships across non-Euclidean network structures, the enormous computational load placed upon the model renders it largely unsuitable for low-power microcontrollers within an embedded IoT environment. Specifically, this literature survey has highlighted 5 critical research voids ignored by current literature: (1) A prevalence of high-performance computing test-beds with insufficient examination of edge hardware compatibility, (2) A complete absence of resource efficiency in model benchmarking (3) Immature model compression approaches that overlook the intrinsic properties of graph structures (4) A lack of standardized graph construction protocols for network traffic and (5) An inherent operational disconnect between the requirements imposed by legal compliance and existing edge hardware capabilities. This work designs, implements, and evaluates a lightweight Graph Neural Network anomaly detection pipeline on real, labelled IoT network traffic, using a standardized graph construction approach and a minimally-parameterized architecture, with resource efficiency assessed analytically against the published hardware profile of a representative constrained edge device. Physical hardware validation is identified as the principal direction for the work that follows.
